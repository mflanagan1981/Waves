ODSL All-Star Field Size Planner

2026 cut-time adjustment model · rankings through the regular season (pre-divisionals) · projections include the divisionals bump, the 3-event limit, and historical attendance

Executive summary

The 2026 All-Star meet is on track to be much smaller than recent years, with uneven event fields. The board’s decision: select a minimum field size per event.

The issue. At the adopted cut times, 2026 projects about 850 swims from roughly 400 swimmers — a fifth smaller than 2024 (1,050 swims) and well under 2025 (992) — and the shrinkage is uneven: 34 of 52 events project below an 18-swimmer field (24 below 16, where scoring places start going empty) while 5 events project 25 or more. The fix is mechanical: raise the cut times of the thin events until every event reaches a minimum field size the board selects.

Factors considered

  • Additional qualifiers at divisionals. Fields grow at divisionals; measured from 2024–25, growth runs from about 2% for 15-18s to about 14% for 6 & Unders, and the 15th–30th ranked times drop by a measured percentage at each position.
  • Swimmers who qualify in 4-5 events. About a quarter of qualifiers exceed the 3-event limit, so raising a cut adds fewer swims than qualifiers; the model assigns each swimmer their 3 highest-ranked events.
  • Qualifiers who decline All Stars. A small share skip the meet — from about 9% of 6 & Unders to about 26% of 15-18s across 2024–25 — applied as expected attendance.
  • Host capacity. All-Star heat sheets seed 6 lanes, so every scenario is expressed in swimmers, swims, heats, and modeled racing time, directly comparable to meets already run.
  • Accuracy. Blind-tested against 2025 using only pre-divisionals data: mean error of about 1.75 swimmers per event.

Appendix — model detail and evidence

The planner projects every event’s All-Star field from three ingredients: who has qualified so far, how much fields historically grow at divisionals, and how many qualifiers actually swim at All Stars. Each ingredient is measured from the 2024 and 2025 seasons, not assumed.

1The 2026 field of performers

The starting point is the current league rankings (Top 50 per event, all 7 regular-season meets). For any candidate cut time, the tool counts the swimmers already at or under it. This is a hard count, not an estimate.

2The divisionals bump — how many qualifiers get added

Divisionals adds qualifiers two ways: ranked swimmers drop time past the cut, and swimmers post a first qualifying swim. We measure the effect two independent ways, comparing the 2024 and 2025 rankings from the week before divisionals against the rankings after.

First, count growth — how many swimmers sat under each event’s cut before divisionals versus after. It is strongly age-dependent and nearly identical across the two seasons:

Second, the ranking-curve drop. Two related currents decide who ends up under a proposed cut: the cut time moves up — the board’s lever, admitting swimmers already ranked just beyond today’s line — and the 15th-through-30th ranked times move down through divisionals, the field’s own improvement carrying swimmers under the line. The second current is measured as a percentage improvement of each ranked position (a percentage of the time, so 25m, 50m, and 100m events pool cleanly). The top of every ranking barely moves — divisionals almost never changes the top 10 — while the compression lives in the middle of the field, exactly the 15–30 band where every cut sits, and most for the youngest groups:

Share of the Nth-ranked time shaved off through divisionals — the smoothed rank-by-rank profile the tool applies, measured on 2024–25, both genders pooled. For intuition: 1% of a 20-second 8&Under swim is about 0.2 seconds; 1% of a 45-second 11-12 breaststroke is about 0.45 seconds. The tool carries the full profile from rank 1 to 50, not just these three points, and applies it multiplicatively to each ranked time.

These are two views of the same movement — one counts swimmers crossing a fixed time, the other watches the time at a fixed rank — and they tell a consistent story: a modest effect league-wide, concentrated in the youngest groups and the deeper ranks. They differ in one behavioral respect: swimmers appear to target the published cuts. Post-divisionals swims pile up disproportionately in the last one percent under the line — the fullest band anywhere near the cut — and 42% of the swimmers who crossed at divisionals in 2024–25 landed within 1% of it. The count view, measured at the line, absorbs that goal-seeking; the curve view is blind to the line by construction and models improvement as if no one knew where it was. The count view is also steadier year to year, while the curve view automatically adapts when a proposed cut sits deeper or shallower than the adopted one. The tool computes the divisionals bump both ways at whatever cut is set and uses the average.

3Yield — what fraction of qualifiers actually swim

Qualifying is not entering. Matching every entry on the 2024 and 2025 All-Star heat sheets back to that season’s qualifier lists lets us account for every single qualification (one swimmer under the cut in one event) and put it in exactly one of four buckets: it became a swim; it was structurally impossible because the swimmer qualified in more than 3 events and had to drop it (about a quarter of qualifiers hit the cap); the swimmer skipped All Stars entirely; or the swimmer attended but swam a lighter program than allowed. League-wide, 75% of qualifications became swims, the 3-event cap removed 15%, skipping removed 10% — and the fourth bucket is essentially empty: swimmers who show up swim everything they’re allowed to. The two loss mechanisms behave differently: for ages 9–12 attendance is excellent and the 3-event cap does nearly all the trimming, while for 15-18 skipping the meet is the single biggest leak. The tool treats them differently too — the cap is applied exactly, swimmer by swimmer, assuming each swims their 3 highest-ranked events (true for 88–91% of entries); the “fill rate” then covers attendance on the slots that survive the cap.

Putting it together

Projected entries = today’s qualifiers, grown by the divisionals rate for that age group, with each projected qualifier swimming their top 3 qualified events at the fill rate for their age. Because of the 3-event limit, the tool recomputes the whole league on every adjustment — loosening one event can pull a swimmer’s third swim away from another event, and the projections reflect that.

Accuracy check. Run against 2025 as a blind test — using only pre-divisionals rankings and 2024 parameters — each method alone projected actual All-Star field sizes with a mean error of about 1.8 swimmers; their average did the same while flagging 21 of the 23 events that truly ran under 18 — two borderline misses, with only a handful of extra flags that finished just over. That average is what the tool runs on.

Technical notes (data, model detail, backtest)

Data. Current 2026 league rankings (Top 50 per event, through the final week of the regular season), the adopted 2026 All-Star cuts, and two full seasons of history: 2024 and 2025 pre-divisional rankings, post-divisional rankings, and All-Star heat sheets.

Model. For each event: count qualifiers under the candidate cut; compute the divisionals bump two ways — count growth for the event’s age group () and a rank-by-rank shift of the current ranking curve using the measured drop profile of the Nth-ranked time — and average them; assign each current qualifier their top 3 qualified events by rank; multiply each assignment by the fill rate for the swimmer’s age group. Divisionals additions are treated as single-event qualifiers entering at the event’s fill rate. The whole league recomputes on every adjustment, so cross-event displacement from the 3-event limit is captured. Because rankings are cumulative bests, the curve shift is structurally one-directional: the Nth time can only get faster. The expansion rows name this view’s crossers: the divisionals bubble lists every swimmer whose current time sits within their rank’s expected drop of the cut. Projections are compared as displayed — rounded to whole swimmers — for status, suggestions, and counts alike, crediting the expected fractional swimmer; the backtest’s conservative bias (about 0.7 swimmers of underprojection) makes that credit empirically safe. Each swimmer’s event preference order — rank position first, then time relative to the adopted cut — is precomputed once and held fixed; any proposed cut vector is resolved against that order, and “Suggest” iterates the whole league to a fixed point where no event’s suggestion changes, so the result is self-consistent and running it again changes nothing.

Swimmer detail rows. Expanding an event lists its qualifiers under the proposed cut in rank order. A struck-through swimmer is projected to spend all 3 swims on higher-ranked events; the note shows those events and the swimmer’s rank in each. Attendance is probabilistic, so expected no-shows are stated as a count rather than pinned to names. Swimmers who qualify under the proposed cut but not the adopted cut are tagged NEW.

Meet time. Heats of 6 seeded fastest-together — every ODSL All-Star heat sheet from 2023 through 2025 seeds 6 lanes — with each heat costing its slowest seed time plus 30 seconds. Projected entries beyond the ranked list (divisionals additions) are assumed to seed at the cut time. Projected heat counts assume full heats; the 2024–25 sheets actually printed about 10% more heats than that floor, from scratches and seeding gaps, so hosts should pad accordingly. Totals exclude breaks and ceremonies, so use the difference between proposed and adopted, which is robust to those exclusions.

Backtest. Given only 2025 pre-divisional rankings and 2024-derived parameters, the blended pipeline projected actual 2025 All-Star field sizes with a mean error of 1.75 swimmers (37 of 45 measurable events within ±3) and flagged 21 of the 23 events that ran below the 18-swimmer standard (14 of the 16 below 16). Projections for events with 50+ current qualifiers are lower bounds, shown with a ≥ sign.

Reading the table. The colored Projected All-Star Entries number carries the field verdict: red below the target minimum, green from the target through 24 (the top of ODSL’s historical 16–24 range; four full 6-lane heats), blue 25 or more — a fifth heat, which cannot be reduced mid-season. The Suggested Action column is the recommendation for each event at the current target: Raise Cut Time when the projected field is below the target minimum, No change to cut time when the cut already projects at or above it, and Cut Time Raised once a proposed raise has brought the event to the target — the amount is the difference between the Adopted and Proposed cut columns. “Suggest” finds the smallest loosening that brings an event below standard up to the target; candidate cuts include every 0.01-second point where the projection can change — each ranked time and each projected divisionals crossing — so the cut is dialed to the exact target whenever one exists. When a tie at the line or an exhausted ranked list makes the exact target unreachable, Suggest settles on the nearest achievable field and says why beneath the proposed cut.
